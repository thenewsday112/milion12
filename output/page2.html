Placing very long branch taxa in the plant tree of life: A case study with Dioscoreales mycoheterotrophs

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise The inclusion of heterotrophic plants in broader systematic studies is challenging due to substantial morphological modification, gene loss, and accelerated substitution rates following photosynthesis loss. In particular, inclusion of very long branch (VLB) taxa with highly elevated rates may lead to phylogenomic misinference. Methods We explored how rapidly evolving plastomes behave in model‐based phylogenetic inferences in the yam order Dioscoreales, which experienced convergent photosynthesis losses. Our taxon sampling included mycoheterotrophic lineages displaying a broad range of substitution rates and all photosynthetic genera. Results When only moderately rapidly evolving heterotrophs are included, relationships within Dioscoreales are congruent with recent mitochondrial analysis, and generally strongly supported. Photosynthetic Stenomeris (represented by only three genes) is moderately supported as sister to Dioscoreaceae. Notably, Burmanniaceae and Thismiaceae, two mycoheterotrophic families including highly reduced plastomes, are strongly supported as distantly related within Dioscoreales. Constrained analysis rejects combining Thismiaceae with Burmanniaceae, conflicting with current angiosperm classification. Including a subset of VLB mycoheterotrophs in phylogenetic inference results in localized or broad reduction in branch support—substantially so for Afrothismiaceae, a family placing in disparate locations in Dioscoreales (and beyond) in variant analyses, with no ability to differentiate among its alternative placements. Conclusions Inclusion of highly rate‐elevated taxa in analyses of Dioscoreales phylogeny can depress branch support. Inferred relationships in the order are otherwise congruent with studies based on mitochondrial data; the family‐level classification needs updating. The extraordinarily rapidly evolving family Afrothismiaceae places inconsistently in diverse plastid analyses, pointing to probable analytical limits for plastid‐based phylogenomic analyses of VLB heterotrophs.

Evolutionary relationship between organellar DNA inheritance patterns and traits of seed plants

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise In plants, organellar DNA is predominantly maternally inherited, but biparental and paternal inheritance have also been observed. Specific mechanisms, such as plastid exclusion during pollen mitosis or genome degradation, ensure maternal inheritance. Certain factors, including low temperatures, disrupt cytoskeletal functions, thereby increasing paternal plastid transmission. Despite these findings, the ecological and evolutionary drivers of these inheritance modes remain unclear. We explored the traits associated with biparental and paternal inheritance to reveal the contributing ecological factors. Methods Plastid inheritance data were compiled for 378 species (302 genera and 112 families) and 12 functional traits obtained from the TRY database were analyzed. Missing values were filled according to Bayesian hierarchical modeling. On the basis of phylogenetic logistic regression, we investigated the relationships between inheritance patterns and plant traits, accounting for evolutionary history. Results Our models identified plant height, leaf area, and stem conduit density as traits associated with inheritance modes, with the strongest support for leaf area and limited support for the other traits. Coefficient directions indicated greater plant height, smaller leaves, and higher stem conduit density in species with non‐maternal inheritance. Excluding gymnosperms yielded similar tendencies, which suggests that similar ecological factors may be associated with plastid inheritance patterns in both groups. Conclusions Functional traits were associated with organelle inheritance patterns, with ecological and evolutionary implications. Similar ecological factors may be associated with the independent occurrence of paternal inheritance across lineages. Future work integrating molecular and ecological data will help elucidate the mechanisms underlying organelle inheritance and their adaptive significance.

Modeling diseased xylem networks

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
Abstract Vascular wilt pathogens induce xylem blockages that disrupt plant water transport, posing critical threats to agricultural productivity and food security. Key processes including xylem transport, vessel blockage, and pathogen spread within the xylem remain poorly understood, largely due to our limited ability to internally monitor infected tissues. This review explores how complementary mathematical modeling alongside laboratory studies can elucidate features governing these critical processes. I first provide a brief history of xylem imaging and quantification, highlighting technological advances, and persistent limitations. I then review modeling approaches to describe xylem water transport and pathogen dynamics, emphasizing that different pathogens require fundamentally different modeling frameworks. Existing xylem flow models may provide suitable hydraulic conductivity estimates for healthy stems under idealized conditions; however, they lack explicit representation of pit membranes and lumen‐occluding structures, limiting their applicability to diseased stems where these features are actively modified during infection. Previously described biofilm models can replicate bacterial colony growth in controlled experimental settings but are often too complex for integration with flow simulations and rely on parameters that are difficult to quantify experimentally. Finally, I highlight directions and challenges for modeling occlusion and spread at the local scale and discuss how these models might be scaled to network‐level architecture. While these types of models remain computationally challenging, advances in multiscale imaging technologies and scaling approaches promise significant progress in the coming decades. Insights from such models stand to advance both our understanding of disease progression and the development of resilient crops for an uncertain climate future.

Plants are incredibly diverse, and so are botanists! In its mission to spread fascinating stories about the plant world, Botany One also introduces you to the scientists behind these great stories.

Today, we have Dr Sean Hoban. Hoban thinks of himself as a conservation biologist first: he wants to understand how plants and their populations work, but what he ultimately seeks is to see that knowledge used in the real world. His team integrates ideas and tools from botany, evolution, and ecology to help conserve plants both in situ and ex situ.

Hoban is a Senior Scientist and Tree Conservation Biologist at The Morton Arboretum near Chicago. The Arboretum has been researching and conserving trees for more than a hundred years, while also working with communities and educators to foster a love of trees and to share knowledge about tending to them, so it’s a good home for me. His research includes understanding small and declining populations, identifying threats to genetic diversity, prioritising plant species for conservation, and figuring out how well seed banks and botanic gardens conserve diversity. Hoban is also interested in seed dispersal, biogeography, and how forests respond to increasing drought and heat.

One of the most central areas of Hoban's work focuses on translating conservation science into practical tools, including conservation gap analyses for botanic gardens and indicators for monitoring genetic diversity at national and global scales, such as under the Convention on Biological Diversity. You can follow his work at his lab website, LinkedIn and Bluesky.

What made you become interested in plants?

I think it started with two things happening when I was young. One was becoming aware of the deforestation crisis. Amazon deforestation was frequently in the news when I was growing up, and I remember my shock at the scale of what was being lost. It was probably my first realisation that something as enormous and vital for life as a forest could be threatened by humans.

At the same time, I did a lot of hiking and camping in my teenage years. I fell in love with being under a tree canopy and started paying attention to the trees themselves, not just forests as scenery, but individual species and how different they could be. I became interested in looking closely at trees and thinking about how they survive: how they reproduce, tolerate stressful conditions, and persist for such long periods.

Those two interests, fascination with forests and concern about their loss, came together in my interest in plant conservation biology.

Hoban with a Quercus welshii individual in deep sand in Utah in 2017. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What motivated you to pursue your current area of research?

I’ve always been drawn toward applied science. I love understanding how the natural world works, but I also want science to be in the service of society. I want to help us become better caretakers of the world around us. That motivation has shaped the questions I choose and the scale at which I work.

It is also why a significant part of my research has moved toward conservation policy and developing tools that other people can actually use. For example, I’ve worked on conservation gap analyses that help identify which species and populations are not yet sufficiently protected by botanical gardens. I also helped create, test, improve, and train people on how to work with indicators of genetic diversity that countries can use to monitor and then take action to restore nature, under national and international commitments.

I love being a part of the process of moving from scientific knowledge to conservation action. And I love bringing more academic scientists out into the policy and management space. We can learn an enormous amount about genetic diversity, ecology, and threats to species, but ultimately we need to turn that knowledge into decisions: What should we conserve? How? And how can we tell whether our efforts are working?

Hoban in his role as a global conservation policy expert, attending the Convention on Biodiversity meeting in Cali, Colombia, in 2025. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What is your favourite part of your work related to plants?

I love seeds. I am fascinated by seeing a plant produce seeds, watching how those seeds disperse, and then seeing seedlings establish and grow. A seed is such a small piece of life, but inside it is the species’ genetic inheritance and the possibility of a whole new plant.

Seeds also connect many parts of my research. Dispersal determines how populations are connected across landscapes, and across time, from years to millennia. Seed collection is one of our important tools for ex situ conservation and something I’ve been privileged to take part in, actually helping add to the living collections at The Morton Arboretum. And regeneration in wild populations, whether seedlings can establish and survive, is fundamental to whether a population or forest will persist into the future.

There is also something wonderful about seeing and holding (and planting) seeds. A lot of my work involves genetic data, computer models, large datasets, and conservation policy, which can become abstract. Finding seeds, or seeing seedlings emerge, brings everything back to the plant itself. It is a reminder of what the analyses and conservation plans are intended to protect.

Sean holding two seeds of the threatened butternut tree in 2024, a finding that sparked subsequent intensive research into the ecology of seed production and regeneration of this tree. Photo by Sean Hoban.

Are any specific plants or species that have intrigued or inspired your research? If so, what are they and why?

I’ve worked intensively with the butternut tree (Juglans cinerea) for 20 years, and it is a tree that continues to inspire me. Butternut is a North American tree with considerable ecological and historical importance, but its populations have declined severely because of an introduced fungal disease, butternut canker.

What fascinates me is that butternut is a survivor. The disease and decline situation is serious, but unlike the American chestnut, butternut has not been devastated to quite the same degree. Mature trees still survive, reproduce, and, under the right conditions, can thrive. That means there is real potential for conservation and restoration.

A lot of our team’s and our collaborators’ work now asks what those " right conditions " are. How much light does butternut need? What forest management practices help it regenerate? What soils and habitats are best? And how much of survival and disease response is related to genetics?

I like working on a species where there are difficult conservation problems but also genuine reasons for optimism. With enough knowledge and effort, we have an opportunity to help this tree persist and recover. If you are reading this and interested in participating, you can be part of it!

Hoban assessing the health of a Juglans cinerea seedling in Illinois. Photo by Sean Hoban.

Could you share an experience or anecdote from your work that has marked your career and reaffirmed your fascination with plants?

One of the things I’ve enjoyed most about working on butternut is not a single field experience, but the community of people around the tree. I’ve had the opportunity to work with an extraordinary variety of people: researchers, students, retired foresters, land managers, naturalists, and people who love the land for agriculture or for bird watching, as well as Martin Kratt, one of the Kratt Brothers, who has become involved in butternut conservation.

Two colleagues of mine now lead a monthly conference call that brings together about 30 butternut researchers and enthusiasts across the continent. Some people have spent decades working with this species and bring an incredible depth of knowledge; others are students or people encountering these questions for the first time. It is a very supportive and affirming group, united by wanting to understand and conserve this tree.

That experience has reminded me that conservation is collaborative. No single researcher or institution has all the knowledge needed to save a species. Plants can bring together people with very different backgrounds and expertise, sometimes over generations. The community and our shared sense that we might help butternut recover is continually rewarding and reaffirming.

Hoban assessing the health of a Juglans cinerea seedling in Illinois, 2025. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What advice would you give young scientists considering a career in plant biology?

Be interdisciplinary. Some of the most interesting and important questions in plant biology are between traditional fields, and many conservation problems cannot be solved from a single perspective.

My own work has taken me through genetics, ecology, botany, computer modeling, biogeography, fieldwork, seed conservation, and eventually policy. I didn’t begin my career knowing that I would work across all of those areas. Often I learned something because it was the tool I needed to answer the next question.

I would encourage young scientists to develop strong expertise in something, but not let that expertise be a boundary. Learn quantitative and computational skills, but also learn natural history. Spend time in the field. Talk to people in other disciplines. Learn how conservation practitioners and policymakers think about problems by talking to them in person. And learn to communicate your science to people who don’t share your speciality.

Be widely curious. Being willing to cross disciplinary boundaries can lead you to questions and new areas of work that you would never have anticipated.

Hoban with Quercus havardii in Texas. This species is adapted to sand dunes and typically grows to about half a meter tall. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What do people usually get wrong about plants?

I think people often underestimate how much diversity exists within a plant species. We tend to think of a species as a single thing: a white oak is a white oak, and conserving the species means making sure enough individuals survive. But individuals and populations can be genetically very different from one another. A population at the dry edge of a species’ range may contain different adaptations from one growing in a cooler or wetter environment. Losing populations can therefore mean losing unique genetic diversity even when the species itself has not gone extinct.

I experienced this recently when seeing beech trees from across Europe in a common garden. Their leaf colour, growth form, and response to drought varied dramatically by population! That matters so much in a rapidly changing world. Conservation isn’t only about keeping species on the planet; it is also about maintaining enough variation within species for them to remain resilient and continue evolving.

I also think plants are often perceived as passive. But when you study their genetics, reproduction, behaviour, responses, and movement across landscapes, you see dynamic stories: migration, adaptation, hybridisation, population expansion and contraction, and exchanges of genes over large distances. A forest may look still, but biologically and evolutionarily it is living and changing.

Drought-Tolerant Perennials for Mid-Atlantic Gardens

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Tough plants that stand up to dry conditions
📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Often overlooked, seeds are an important life stage in the growth of boreal forests. Before a tree is capable of photosynthesis, the germinating seed supports plant growth using stored reserves of carbon, starch, nitrogen, and phosphorus. But higher latitudes are known to adversely affect the deposition of these nutrients within the seed.

This is because higher latitudes have lower nitrogen and phosphorus availability, are colder, have a shorter growing season, and have an increased incidence of fire. And these limitations negatively impact seed production and germination success.

Now, you might think this negative is a negative. But a new study on the reproductive traits of black spruce (Picea mariana) across a latitudinal gradient has shown that smaller seeds and poor germination likely confer special advantages to reproduction in this species.

"In this study, we investigated the variation in cone and seed traits of black spruces growing in five natural sites along a latitudinal gradient from 48°N to 53°N in Quebec, Canada," write Karki and colleagues. Black spruce is a dominant conifer species in North American boreal forests.

This map shows where sample cones were collected from the five black spruce populations studied. SIM, Simoncouche; BER, Bernatchez; MIS, Mistassibi; DAN, Camp Daniel; MIR, Mirage. Figure 1 of Karki et al CC BY

For their study, Karki and colleagues collected 90 fully mature brown cones from each of 10-13 adult black spruce trees growing in the five independent test sites. The researchers then studied cone and seed size, seed number, nutrient composition, and germination rates, comparing the data between latitudes.

They found a clear trend along the latitudinal gradient. Black spruce trees in more southern latitudes have larger seeds, heavier seeds, more seeds per cone, wider cones, and a higher sucrose but lower starch content than more northern populations.

Black Spruce (Picea mariana) growing in Charlevoix-Est, Québec, Canada. The cones and seeds get smaller the farther north they grow. Image Credit: R. LLM via iNaturalist CC BY-NC 4.0

Karki and colleagues concluded from these data that the heavier seeds produced in southern areas are packed with nutrients that allow them to grow well in place, while lighter seeds of northern areas can be dispersed by the wind to new locations. Both confer advantages to the seed.

That's because these differences affect germination. Where its warmer, in the southern latitudes, the seed can drop, full of nutrients, and establish a new tree. But in higher latitudes, fewer nutrients in the seeds leads to lower, and delayed, germination, which may sound bad, but is really an advantage. The delay prevents seeds from germinating when there's still a risk for frost, helping to ensure the survival of the next generation. Fewer nutrients also result in a light seed that can be carried by the wind to new, favourable growing locations.

Northern black spruce cones also appear to be better suited to fire ecology; the cones need more heat to open than their southern relations. This "could reflect an adaptation to the shorter fire intervals occurring at higher latitudes" write Karki and colleagues. This is important because climate change is predicted to shorten the fire intervals in Eastern Canada. And so having cones that can withstand fire and protect their seeds helps mitigate risks to the next generation.

Together, these results paint a hopeful picture. Black spruce may have built-in resiliency along the latitudinal gradient that allows the boreal forest to adapt to changing conditions over time.

Do spiders dream? Scientists just found a surprising clue

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Scientists have discovered that spiders may sleep in ways surprisingly similar to humans, complete with distinct sleep stages and a dream-like REM state. Researchers at the University of Bonn studied the Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata, a remarkably clever hunter known for its sophisticated strategies. By recording the spiders over several days and nights, they found clear evidence of regulated sleep, including a REM-like stage marked by eye movements and muscle twitches.

The Business of Flower Farming: Bewitching Blooms

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
The farmhouse could wait; Laura Grimm had 11 acres to reclaim and a flower farm to build
📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When you walk through the Brazilian Cerrado and look down at the ground, you might find a myriad of flowers blooming within a single square metre. Take five more steps, and that diversity may have completely changed. Two weeks later, in that very same spot, the earlier blooms may well have vanished to make way for entirely new species. In the Cerrado, plant life does not unfold at an even pace. It is a dynamic dance orchestrated by two grand conductors: time and fire.

A study published in Annals of Botany showed how the taxonomic, functional, and phylogenetic diversity of bee-pollinated plants and their interactions with bees are structured across time and space, analysing data from 1,200 small 1 m² plots over two years. The aim was to determine what drives greater change in floral display and bee visits: the physical distance between patches or the passing of weeks? And what happens when a fire sweeps through this mosaic?

The Brazilian Cerrado. Photo by Helena Lopes from Pexels.

Distance did not explain the differences between the flowers. Two neighbouring square metres could be just as different from one another as two points hundreds of metres apart. The true conductor of change is time. The Cerrado's rapid phenological succession means that the composition of flowering plant species shifts continuously every few weeks, ensuring a steady, ever-renewing supply of nectar and pollen for pollinators.

Yet when a fire struck part of the study area, the researchers were able to compare the exact “before and after”. The fire broke dormancy and stimulated the flowering of several adapted species, increasing the likelihood of finding blooms scattered across the terrain. However, there was a decrease in the overall abundance of flowers, owing to bud damage on woody species such as trees and shrubs. Even so, the diversity of floral shapes and evolutionary lineages remained stable, ensuring that varied types of resources continued to be available for bees. The bees responded swiftly to this altered landscape. Although the number of visits per flower dropped temporarily, the bees began foraging across the terrain in a much broader, more evenly distributed manner.

Fire across the Cerrado landscape. Photo by Arthur de Magalhães Goulart (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0).

Furthermore, fire reduced the abundance of tree flowers, curtailed the dominance of the honeybee (Apis mellifera), creating space and opportunities for diverse solitary and social native bees to visit Cerrado flowers, giving native species a fighting chance.

Conserving the Cerrado calls for an understanding of the harmony within this vast ecological symphony. It is a duet between time and fire where every movement matters: the coexistence of burned and unburned patches, the rapid turnover of flowers week by week, and the many other factors explored across ecological research together compose the arrangement that keeps the dance of native pollinators fine-tuned and resilient, conducting the continuous entrance of new floral instruments.

I'm going to be a bit unusual with the Introduction this week because it's been an unusual week – the sports pages broke a botanical story about a newly discovered plant species that is endemic to the Andes of Peru.

Researchers identified a new species of Salvia, and because the flower's colouring is similar to the Argentinean flag, they decided to honour Lionel Messi, widely regarded as Argentina's best football player of all time, who retired from international competition after this year's World Cup, by naming the species after him.

Sports enthusiasts learned that the new species will be called Salvia messii. And the announcement is so recent, I was unable to find a verified picture using the species name. So the unusual bit of the Introduction is that I'm including the lead researcher's Instagram announcement. The video is in Spanish, but even if you don't speak Spanish, the images speak for themselves.

View this post on Instagram

Amazingly, this means Messi has broken his own Guinness World Record for most species named after a football player. His other eponymous species are a lizard, spider, Miocene-era fossil brachiopod, Pliocene-era fossil condor, and a fungus. Sadly, Salvia messii is already considered endangered and in need of conservation. But hopefully its association with the sports world will bring needed attention to the species, its habitat, and conservation efforts generally to a wider, nonacademic audience.

Scientists have discovered a giant new fungus in China that grows up to 20 inches tall and weighs nearly five pounds, making it the largest known of its kind. Dubbed “D-rex,” its evolutionary lineage appears to have emerged shortly after the dinosaurs went extinct, raising intriguing questions about how that ancient catastrophe may have shaped its evolution.

This week’s plant was an easy choice after reading two articles by Erika Alejandra Chaves-Díaz. One explores a medical innovation inspired by the way Drosera capensis captures its prey, while the other reveals how some insects manage to escape the sticky traps of Drosera species. After reading both, I couldn’t resist learning a little more about these fascinating carnivorous plants.

Drosera capensis, commonly known as the Cape sundew, is a carnivorous plant native to South Africa’s Cape region, where it grows in moist habitats such as marshes, streamsides and boggy areas. Its scientific history goes back to 1753, when Carl Linnaeus described it in Species Plantarum, the book that established the modern system of naming plants using two-part scientific names. Since then, this species has continued to attract scientific attention. It was the first sundew to have its genome sequenced and is known to produce several compounds with potential medicinal applications.

Drosera capensis in its natural habitat. Photo by Tony Rebelo (iNaturalist, CC BY-SA 4.0).

But what makes these plants so fascinating? Like other sundews, Drosera capensis has long, narrow leaves covered in tiny glandular hairs that produce glistening droplets of a sticky substance. When an unsuspecting insect comes into contact with these droplets, it can become trapped, triggering the leaf to slowly curl around its prey before releasing digestive enzymes to break it down.

Interestingly, this trapping mechanism fascinated Charles Darwin, who described it in his 1875 book Insectivorous Plants. Darwin observed that while contact with solid objects could cause the sticky tentacles of sundews to bend, falling water droplets did not trigger the same response. More than a century later, researchers studying Drosera capensis discovered that mechanical stimulation and chemical signals from prey activate electrical and hormonal responses that help the plant capture and digest insects. Water droplets, however, fail to activate these responses fully, preventing the plant from wasting energy reacting to every passing shower.

Time-lapse of Drosera capensis eating a mosquito. Total time elapsed is ~1 hour.

Interestingly, the same mechanism that makes Drosera capensis such an effective predator has inspired scientists to develop new medical technologies. As Erika explained in her article earlier this week, researchers have developed a patch with tiny structures that curl when exposed to body temperature, mimicking the movement of sundew leaves to help close wounds. Although this technology is still experimental, it is a remarkable example of how studying plants can inspire solutions to problems that seem completely unrelated to botany.

However, despite their sophisticated trapping mechanisms, sundews are not always successful hunters. Some insects have surprising ways of escaping their sticky leaves, challenging our idea of these plants as inescapable predators. Next week, Erika will introduce us to some of these remarkable escape artists and the strategies they use to avoid becoming a sundew's next meal. Hopefully, by then, you'll be just as fascinated by these little plants as I am!
