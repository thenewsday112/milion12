The Mexican marigold lies at the heart of Dia de los Muertos, a powerful celebration of the lives of the dead that occurs annually in Mexico on 1-2 November. On this ancient holiday, from the time of the Aztecs, the veil between the worlds thins and loved ones return to visit. Marigolds play a pivotal role in their return – the souls use the bright orange colour and musky scent from the leaves and stems as a bright and fragrant path to find their way home.

"In Mexico, we call the marigold cempasúchil, which comes from a Náhuatl word meaning "twenty flowers," shared Ana Valladares, one of our writers here at Botany One, who is from Mexico. "It is one of the many important symbols we use on the Día de los Muertos to decorate graves, altares and map out a glowing trail to welcome our loved ones back home. People are usually surprised by how we connect death with such bright, yellow-orange flowers, but for us, it’s all about duality. It reminds us that death is just a natural part of life's cycle, then you can’t have light -life- without obscurity -death. Its vibrant colors also act as a visual guide, basically lighting up the path so the souls don't get lost."

Left: A family alter to honour the dead (Image: Alberto Lara from Pexels via Canva); Right: A cemetery adorned with candles and marigold flowers (maogg from Getty Images Signature via Canva).

Mexican marigold has a long history of use. It was both foraged and cultivated by the Aztecs for ceremonial, decorative, and medicinal purposes. Today, its use in Dia de los Muertos connects people across time. The festival, with its deep roots in Aztec history, blends Aztec and Roman Catholic traditions. The Aztec goddess Mictecacihuatl, or the Lady of the Dead, allows the spirits to return to earth and visit their families, using the golden path of the marigold. At the same time, the traditions of All Saints Day, which is 1 November, are honoured.

All over Mexico, families set up altars, adorned with marigold garlands as well as photographs of the dead, candles, and the deceased's favourite foods and drink. Special skulls made of sugar, called calaveras, are also common. Additionally, families maintain and repair the graves of their loved ones, and decorate cemeteries with the flower. People also participate in a special type of poetry called calaveras literarias, in which they rhyme light-hearted verses to honour the dead.

Mexican marigolds are used to guide spirits home on the Day of the Dead. Their beautiful composite flowers are bright and fragrant. Image Credit: sofiadanielaperezsan01 via iNaturalist CC BY-NC 4.0

Given Mexican marigold's cultural importance, it isn't surprising that it is native to Central America. The plant reaches peak bloom at this time of year, with its showy flowers readily available for the festival. The plant produces many composite flowers composed of ray florets surrounding a central disc. Colour varies depending on their carotenoid and xanthophyll content, and may be yellow, orange, or red. Both flowers and stems are fragrant, with stems containing resin channels in the bark that release scent when crushed. The stems are used to weave garlands for the altares and cemeteries, and plucked petals are laid out in paths for souls to follow home.

Bridging past and present, light and dark, this beautiful flower connects loved ones during Dia de los Muertos, a celebration of remembrance.

Tonight is Nos Calan Gaeaf, the start of winter in the northern hemisphere. From here on we enter the dark quarter of the year. If you live in the better hemisphere then you get the lightest quarter. Like other, more famous, observances, Nos Calan Gaeaf is a spirit night. Various supernatural entities are said to stalk the countryside. It's a night to fear the unknown and listen for the sound of dogs hunting their prey in the black. You could also send out children to forage for treats, especially if you feel your house is a little crowded and you could use more room.

Folklore gets garbled Wikipedia provides a nice example here. Talking of one ritual, it says: "The boys were instructed to cut 10 leaves of ivy, throw one away and put the other nine under their pillows. Apparently, this allowed the boys to see the future, and if they touched the ivy then they could see witches while asleep." When I think of ivy I think of Hedera helix, a plant that provides late flowers for bees and certainly offers them a future. However, it's more likely that the ivy is ground ivy, Glechoma hederacea, an entirely different plant. People using Hedera helix may feel regret, though not as much regret as those using Toxicodendron radicans.

The Perfect Crime in Slow Motion: The Bugs That Steal From Australia’s Carnivorous Plants

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Surviving in nature sometimes means free-riding on the hard work of others. Carnivorous plants expend an enormous amount of energy producing mucilage and digestive enzymes to capture their food. Yet, nature always finds an opening, like sundew bugs of the Australian genus Setocoris, which have made their homes on the lethal leaves of Drosera and feed on the prey the plant captures with so much effort. But stealing in broad daylight requires a master strategy: knowing exactly when the plant has its traps set.

A new study conducted in the national parks of New South Wales shows that these bugs strategically hatch right when different Drosera species reach their peak lethal capacity, ensuring they never run out of free meals.

A Setocoris bug moving freely among the sticky traps of a carnivorous plant (Drosera barbigera) in Western Australia to feed on the prey captured by the plant. Source: Jean from Perth, Australia, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

In the nutrient-poor soils of Australia, these plants, commonly known as sundews, evolved to become clever hunters. Their leaves function as active flypaper traps, covered with mobile tentacles that secrete drops of sticky mucilage to catch and digest small invertebrates. However, while almost any other insect suffers an agonising death, Setocoris bugs are the exception to the rule. These small insects move with impunity across the sticky traps at all stages of their life cycle and engage in what ecologists call "kleptobiosis": the direct theft of prey captured by their host.

Although this curious association has been known since the bug genus was described in 1951, a mystery remained: the synchronisation of their life cycles. Australian sundews are not lethal year-round due to seasonal periods of dormancy, meaning the window during which they actively catch prey can last only a few weeks.

To understand how the thief survives when the "restaurant" is closed, a team led by researcher Zoe Bloesch examined this phenomenon in a study published in Austral Ecology. The team closely observed the behaviour of an undescribed Setocoris species in Royal National Park. This insect frequents two host species with very different growth forms: Drosera peltata, with small rounded leaves, and Drosera binata, with large, linear lobed leaves.

The two host species studied: Drosera peltata (left), featuring small, rounded leaves with a captured insect, and Drosera binata (right), with its characteristic bifurcated leaves. Each displays distinct morphology and peak sticky periods. Photos by Lee Barber (CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons) and Krzysztof Ziarnek, Kenraiz (CC BY-SA 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons).

The main finding of the study by Bloesch and her team, based on field observations over three months in spring, revealed an astonishing synchrony. The scientists discovered that the bugs do not appear at random; instead, their nymphs are present on the plants precisely when their hosts reach peak leaf stickiness, the period of maximum prey capture capacity. Because Drosera peltata reaches its peak number of sticky leaves earlier in the season (September and October), it was there that the bugs were observed first. Conversely, Drosera binata reaches its peak trap activity slightly later (October and November), perfectly matching the subsequent emergence of bugs on that species. The insect's biological clock seems to meticulously track its host’s menu.

To explain how they achieve such perfect timing, the study's authors propose two scenarios. The first suggests that the bug's eggs share an "environmental clock" with the plant: the same moisture or temperature cues that trigger the Drosera to emerge and produce sticky mucilage also trigger the bug's eclosion. The second scenario points to a clever nomadism. Because the study sites were in proximity, sometimes just 100 meters apart, the bugs might hatch first on the early Drosera peltata and, as that host senesces, move on foot or via short flights to Drosera binata, capitalising on two consecutive windows of continuous prey availability.

A Setocoris bug nymph (still wingless) moving without getting trapped on the sticky tentacles of a Drosera grandiflora in Western Australia. Source: Jean and Fred (CC BY 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons).

These observations open new avenues to investigate a highly specialised interaction. Although we have yet to discover exactly where these bugs oviposit their eggs or what precise cues trigger their emergence, one thing is clear: while the carnivorous plant works tirelessly perfecting its deadly trap, the ingenious Setocoris has mastered the enviable art of arriving just in time for dinner.

Two closely related plant species can have very different reproductive strategies. One might pollinate itself; while the other needs pollen arriving from another flower. That small difference can have strong consequences for how species diverge and remain separate. A new study recently published in AoB Plants explores how differences in pollination help keep two Erysimum species apart. 

For one species to become two, its populations have to slowly stop interbreeding. This is known as reproductive isolation, and it can happen in several ways. Plants may grow in different places, flower at different times, attract different pollinators or fail to produce healthy offspring when they cross. 

But these barriers don’t operate independently. Researchers have often studied these barriers one at a time, leaving a key question: how do they work together to keep species from interbreeding? 

Ana García-Muñoz and colleagues studied two closely related species: Erysimum incanum, which mostly pollinates itself, and Erysimum wilczekianum, which needs pollen from other plants. They checked every step of reproduction to find out what stops the two from interbreeding. 

The results reveal an interesting degree of reproductive isolation. When all barriers were considered together, the researchers calculated a total isolation value of 0.999, indicating almost complete separation. That separation doesn’t come from one big obstacle. It comes mainly from barriers that act before pollen reaches a flower. 

Pollinator behaviour seems to be particularly important. Pollinators clearly preferred E. wilczekianum, so pollen rarely made it from one species to the other. If insects keep visiting the same kind of flower, the chances of cross-fertilisation decline. 

The two species differ in floral traits, particularly flower size, which may influence which flowers pollinators visit and, ultimately, where they carry pollen. In Erysimum, flower size seems to be a characteristic that evolves in response to a species’ way of life and, at the same time, reduces mating with other populations.  

The barriers acting after pollination are more complex. When the researchers examined crosses between the species, they found an important difference in the results. Hybrid seed formation was largely prevented when E. wilczekianum was the maternal parent, whereas crosses in the opposite direction were comparatively successful. Although fewer hybrid seeds germinated, those that survived grew particularly well. The researchers also found no evidence that later generations of hybrids become less healthy or less successful at reproducing.  

Taken together, our results contribute to a broader understanding of how mating system divergence can shape the architecture of reproductive isolation in plants. Rather than acting as a single barrier, mating system differences appear to reorganize multiple components of isolation simultaneously, from pollinator-mediated processes to post-pollination compatibility.

These findings suggest that the two species are still capable of producing viable offspring. Rather than being unable to cross, they are kept apart mainly by differences in their environment and the pollinators they attract.

Experimental manipulation of floral display size does not affect the distribution of paternal diversity in Silene dioica

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise Various factors, including pollinator visitation patterns and pollen carryover, govern paternal diversity and its distribution within maternal plants. Floral display size may have opposite effects on fruit‐ and plant‐level paternal diversity, because plants with large displays may receive more pollinator visits but also more sequential probes per visit. Methods To investigate the effect of floral display size on the distribution of paternal diversity in Silene dioica, we genotyped and assigned paternity for 1320 seeds sampled in several fruits per plant in control and manipulated females—whose flower number was artificially increased—in an experimental population exposed to natural pollination. We estimated within‐fruit paternal diversity and sire profile dissimilarity among fruits and compared these metrics between control and manipulated plants. We then quantified pollen carryover using controlled pollinator visitation assays and pollen counts. Results Most fruits were multiply sired, but we found no effect of floral display size on within‐fruit paternal diversity. Sire profiles were more similar between fruits from the same than from different females, but not more so in manipulated than in control plants. The pollen deposition curve was steep, with an increasing carryover fraction throughout the visitation sequence. Conclusions Our results suggest that the effect of floral display size on pollinator foraging behavior is too weak to affect paternal diversity and its distribution in S. dioica, or that other processes, such as limited pollen carryover and spatially restricted pollen dispersal, blur the effect of pollinator visitation patterns on sire profiles.

Methods We selected Ipomoea microsepala, a species of morning‐glory with a markedly U‐shaped distribution, as a candidate to evaluate potential incipient evolutionary divergence. Results We showed that Ipomoea microsepala achieved its current geographic distribution through historical range expansion by successively colonizing adjacent areas in a fashion that is consistent with a stepping‐stone model and isolation by distance. Plants in these fronts are already genetically distinct, do not exhibit introgression, and use marginally different climate spaces, but lack differences in leaf morphology. Conclusions A geographic perspective continues to be useful to identify and shed light on the process of divergence. Further ecological experimentation with a genomic perspective is necessary to assess the degree to which the differentiation we documented is congruent with early stages of speciation.

Abstract Premise The soil depth of underground storage organs (USOs) of geophytes involves cost–benefit trade‐offs and may relate to USO age, size, and disturbances such as fire and herbivory. Yet determinants of USO depth are poorly studied. We posit that belowground mammalian predation of USOs is a selective force for the positive relationship between USO size and depth. Methods We tested the mammalian predation hypothesis using USO depth, mass, type (bulb, corm, tuber, rhizome) and palatability to human foragers for 1828 geophyte individuals (153 species, 54 genera, 22 families) from the lowlands of the Cape Floristic Region (South Africa) which harbors the world's highest concentration of geophytes. Results USOs were found at depths of 0–34 cm, but depth had no consistent relationship with mass. The depth–mass relationship was instead related to USO type: Compared with smaller counterparts, larger bulbs were at shallower depths, and larger corms were deeper. USO palatability affected the depth–mass relationship; palatability was generally associated with deeper burial across USO types: Palatable corms, tubers, and rhizomes mostly had positive depth–mass relationships, and unpalatable types had no relationship. Palatable bulbs had negative relationships that were less significant than for unpalatable bulbs. The phylogenetic signal in the study species was only moderate. Conclusions USO type and palatability explained some variation in their depth–mass relationship in accordance with the mammalian predation hypothesis. USO age and shape, soil type, and predation strategy and intensity should be explored as potential additional correlates of USO depth and size.

Geographic isolation promoted species divergence of two closely related Cycas species in distinct river basins

Abstract Premise Understanding the process of species divergence and the mechanisms that drive genetic differentiation among closely related species is essential for explaining the origins of plant biodiversity. Geographic isolation and niche differentiation are key drivers of species divergence. Cycas chenii and C. guizhouensis are morphologically similar sister species from different river basins in Southwest China. Their distinct habitats in nearby river systems make them a potentially good model system for studying species divergence. Methods We used double‐digest restriction‐site‐associated DNA sequencing data and analyzed the data with a range of analyses to investigate the genetic divergence of C. guizhouensis and C. chenii and to assess the significance of geographic factors and ecological differentiation in their divergence. Results The two species had significant genetic differentiation (genetic differentiation index = 0.34). On the basis of phylogenetic and admixture analyses, the two species are genetically distinct lineages that diverged in the early Miocene. They experienced similar demographic histories over the past several million years. The genetic distance between the two species correlated positively with geographic distance but not with environmental distance. In a niche identity test, the ecological niches of the two species were statistically indistinguishable (Schoener's D = 0.68, I = 0.91, both within the null distribution). Conclusions Environmental variation is unlikely to have been the primary factor driving divergence of C. chenii and C. guizhouensis. Geographic separation instead may have been triggered by shifts in river channels during the early Miocene epoch, playing a pivotal role in interspecific differentiation.

From ice age to present: Clonal expansion and genetic variation in the world's oldest conifer stand

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise Clonality is widespread among plants, yet the processes governing genetic diversity within the most ancient clones remains understudied. We investigated these dynamics using a reputed 10,500‐year‐old clonal stand of one of the world's longest‐lived tree species, the paleoendemic conifer Lagarostrobos franklinii at Mt Read in Western Tasmania. Methods To quantify the clone's spatial extent and map its underlying genetic structure and gain insights into its origin, we used unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) photogrammetry and two complementary genetic data sets including nuclear simple sequence repeats (nSSRs) and multiplexed intersimple sequence repeat genotyping by sequencing (MIG‐seq), a reduced representation SNP discovery method. Results The putative clonal stand at Mt Read has been severely impacted by fire, with living stems occupying approximately 5260 m2, a ~65% reduction in the stand's previously estimated size. Contrary to the hypothesis that a single clone endures at Mt Read, both data sets revealed multiple, circumscribed patches, each with fixed multilocus genotypes (MLGs). Conclusions The Mt Read stand comprises three closely related MLGs, with the two largest clones being 3350 m2 and 1861 m2, respectively, they remain some of the largest conifer clones known. Although extremely low allelic diversity in the stand meant that nSSRs could not exclude a somatic origin for these MLGs, higher resolution MIG‐seq markers indicate an inbreeding origin. Overall, this study demonstrates how ancient clones previously regarded as single genets can harbor cryptic diversity. Accounting for this hidden structure is crucial to accurately estimate clone size and longevity and unravel their evolutionary history.
